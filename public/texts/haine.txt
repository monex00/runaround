The cameras always arrive after the fire. They come in a van with a satellite dish on the roof, they park at the edge of the estate where the street lights still work, and they film what is left: a burnt car, a broken shop window, a line of riot shields across an empty road. Then they leave before the night ends, and the people who live there watch themselves on the evening news, in pictures they did not choose, under words they did not write.

Nobody in the picture is ever asked what happened before. Not the night before, not the year before, not the twenty years of closed doors that came before that. The image starts with the flames. Everything else is left out of frame, and what is left out of frame does not exist. A boy is hurt in a police station and the story is a riot. A riot is shown and the story is the boy who threw the stone. The question of who looks and who is looked at is settled before the first question is asked.

There is an old story about a man who falls from a fifty-storey building. On his way down, to reassure himself, he keeps telling himself: so far, so good. So far, so good. So far, so good. But what matters is not the fall. It is the landing. A society can tell itself the same thing for a very long time, one evening bulletin at a time, as long as the ground is still far away.

The young men on the rooftop know the cameras by now. They know the reporter will lean out of the car window and ask them if they are the ones who burned the gym, and they know that whatever they answer will be cut to fit. So they shout, or they laugh, or they turn their backs, and that is what goes on the air: angry faces, raised fists, a gesture at the lens. The camera never films itself. It never shows the car it came in, the window it leaned out of, the distance it kept.

Above the motorway there is a billboard with a picture of the Earth and a promise in large white letters: the world is yours. Someone has climbed up in the night with a can of paint and corrected it by one letter. The world is ours. It is a small change. It is also the whole argument. A promise made to you is a promise someone else can take back. A world that is ours has to be shared by everyone standing in it, including the ones who were never asked.

Article 10. No one shall be troubled for his opinions, even religious ones, provided that their expression does not disturb the public order established by law.

Article 11. The free communication of thoughts and opinions is one of the most precious rights of man. Every citizen may therefore speak, write and print freely, being answerable for the abuse of this freedom in the cases determined by law.

Article 12. The guarantee of the rights of man and of the citizen requires a public force. This force is therefore established for the benefit of all, and not for the particular use of those to whom it is entrusted.

Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, 26 August 1789.

Article twelve is the shortest of the three and the one that is easiest to forget. The public force is for the benefit of all. Not for the comfort of those who carry it, not for the ratings of those who film it, not for the speeches of those who will explain it the next morning. When the force and the cameras arrive together, and leave together, and tell the same story, the people in the picture learn that the article was not written for them.

Every few years the same images come back. The same burnt cars, the same shields, the same helicopter shots of the same tower blocks at night, the same experts in the same studios. The faces change. The frame does not. Each time, the question on the screen is how to restore order, and each time the question nobody films is what order meant there in the first place, and for whom.

A newspaper page is also a frame. It decides what is printed in large letters and what is left in the small ones at the bottom, what is a headline and what is a footnote. Read this page slowly. Look at what the text has to bend around to keep going, and at what it is not allowed to touch. The figures in the middle of it are not illustrations. They are the story the columns were written to step around.

So far, so good. So far, so good. It is not the fall that matters. It is the landing.
