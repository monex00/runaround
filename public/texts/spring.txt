The thaw comes to the high valleys late, and it never comes all at once. For weeks the snow keeps its grip on the north faces while the south slopes darken in patches, first a stone, then a boulder, then a whole shoulder of the mountain showing through like the back of an animal waking from a long sleep. The people who live up there do not trust the first warm day. They have seen it too many times: a morning of soft air and dripping eaves, then a week of wind that brings the winter back down the passes as if it had only gone to fetch something it forgot.

Still, there is a moment every year when the doubt runs out. Nobody announces it. A shepherd notices that the stream under the bridge has changed its voice, lower and fuller, carrying the sound of meltwater from places no one can see. A child finds the first crocus pushing through the old grass beside the path to the upper pasture, purple against the brown, and runs home to tell, and is not believed until the grown-ups go and look for themselves. The dogs know before anyone. They stand at the edge of the village with their noses lifted, reading the wind, and then one morning they simply set off uphill and do not come back until dark, muddy to the shoulders and wildly pleased with themselves.

There is an old habit in those valleys of walking up to the first ridge on the day the snow lets go of the path. It is not a festival and there are no songs for it. People go alone, or two by two, carrying a stick cut from the hazel by the river and a heel of bread in a pocket. They climb until the village is a handful of roofs below them and the valley opens out into the long view of peaks that have been hidden behind cloud since the autumn. Then they sit on a warm rock for a while and do nothing at all. Ask them why and they will shrug. To see if it is all still there, one woman said once, as though the mountains might have wandered off during the winter.

The young ones take it more seriously than anyone. For a child the walk to the ridge is the first real journey of the year, the first time the world is wide again after months of kitchens and stables and the same three lanes. They plan it for days. They argue about which path is faster and which is drier and whether the old bridge will hold. They polish their sticks and carve marks into them, one for every year they have made the climb, and compare the marks with a fierce and private pride. A stick with five notches is a serious thing. A stick with ten belongs to someone who is nearly grown and will soon stop climbing for the fun of it.

And always there is a dog. It is hard to imagine the walk without one. The dog runs ahead and comes back, runs ahead and comes back, covering the mountain three times over while the child covers it once. It finds every marmot burrow and every patch of old snow worth rolling in. It drinks from every stream as though it had never seen water before. At the top it will not sit still on the warm rock like the people do; it stands on the highest point it can find, ears up, and stares out at the valley with an expression of tremendous importance, as if it had personally arranged the view and wanted to make sure that nothing had been moved.

The view repays the effort. From the first ridge on a clear spring morning you can see the whole chain of the range laid out in order, the near peaks dark with forest and wet rock, the far ones still white and so sharp against the sky that they look cut from paper. Cloud lies in the deep valleys like milk in a bowl. Far below, the river is a thread of light. Somewhere a cowbell sounds, too distant to place, and the sound arrives a little after the movement that made it, so that the whole landscape seems to be keeping its own slow time, a beat behind the world.

The old people say the mountain gives back what you bring to it. Come up angry and you will find a cold wind and loose stones. Come up tired and every step will be a quarrel. But come up on the right morning, with a good stick and a dog that will not stop running, and the mountain gives you the whole of the spring at once, all the weeks of slow melting folded into one bright hour. That is why they keep the habit, even now that the young leave for the cities and the pastures grow thinner every year. You do not climb to the ridge to get anywhere. You climb to be there when the year turns over.

Coming down is always faster. The dog leads the way, tired at last, trotting with its tongue out and looking back every few yards to make sure it has not been abandoned. The light goes gold and then copper on the peaks behind. In the village the first lamps are lit and the smell of wood smoke drifts up the path to meet the walkers before they can see the roofs. Supper is waiting. Someone asks how it was up there and the answer is always the same, a shrug and a smile and a single word: still there.

Later, when the child is asleep and the stick with its new notch is leaning in the corner by the door, the dog lies across the threshold with one ear turned to the mountain. It dreams, and its legs move a little, running uphill in its sleep. Outside the meltwater goes on talking under the bridge, lower and fuller than it did last night, carrying the winter away down the valley one cold drop at a time. In the morning there will be more grass showing, and more stones, and the first bees working the crocus by the path. The year has turned over. It will not turn back now.
